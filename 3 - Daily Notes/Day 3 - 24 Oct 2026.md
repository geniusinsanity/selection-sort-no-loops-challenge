## Entry 8: The Floor Tiles (Carrelage) Revelation!

- **Time:** Afternoon
- **Tool / medium:** Real life observation & AI Search
- **Place / source:** University hallway & AI discussion
- **Goal:** Find what the teacher really meant by "the algorithm contains booleans" in a PhD exam.
- **The Story:**
  I was walking down the university hallway, thinking hard about the teacher's hint ("booleans").
  I looked down at the floor and saw the square tiles (*le carrelage*). They formed a perfect $N \times N$ grid.
  Suddenly, an idea hit me:
  > Why are we thinking in a straight line with sequential loops?
  > If we have $N$ numbers, what if we lay them out on a grid of $N \times N$ tiles?
  > Every tile $(i, j)$ can be a simple **boolean comparison**: Is $A[i] > A[j]$?
  > If we count how many `true` values are in row $i$, that number is the EXACT final sorted index (rank) of $A[i]$!
- **Research & Video Search:**
  I asked the AI about this idea and searched on YouTube.
  I discovered that this algorithm is formally called:
  **"Parallel Enumeration Sort"** or **"Rank Sort using a Boolean Comparison Matrix"**!
  It is a famous textbook problem in advanced PRAM parallel computing and PhD algorithm exams!
  This explains why the teacher said:
  1. It contains booleans (every cell in the matrix is a boolean!).
  2. No loops or recursion needed (in theory/PRAM, all cells are evaluated at once).
  3. It was from a PhD exam!

  3. **[final_matrix_sort.cpp](file:///home/wsm/Desktop/Selection%20Sort%20Project/4%20-%20The%20Program/final_matrix_sort.cpp):**
     Combined everything into a master program with a `MatrixPrinter` so there are zero loops in the entire file. All 6 test cases passed!
- **Key Insight about Pseudo-code vs C++:**
  In C++, we used constructors to allocate the $N \times N$ cells without writing `for` loops.
  However, in **Pseudo-code** (the pure theoretical solution), the algorithm is general and doesn't need any language tricks, because all comparisons $(i, j)$ are computed simultaneously in mathematical set notation!
